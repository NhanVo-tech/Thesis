Smart Car Access · System Overview

Your phone is the key.
The car knows when you mean it.

Walk up to the driver's door and it unlocks. Sit down and the engine is allowed to start. Walk away and the car locks itself. There are no buttons to press and no fob in your pocket, just an Android phone, three radio anchors and a small neural network running inside the car.

Overview

The big picture

Four players work together. The phone is the key. The car computer, an ESP32-S3 microcontroller, is the lock that thinks. Three UWB anchors on the car act as its eyes, measuring exactly how far away the phone is. The cloud keeps the account, the car list and a history that is watched for suspicious use. Everything in this document fits into one of these four steps. Pairing happens once per phone. The other three happen every time you walk up to the car.

03
Step 1 · NFC · once per phone

Pairing: teaching the car to trust your phone

Before a phone can open the car, the car has to learn who it is. The owner first scans the NFC master card that belongs to the car, which prepares the app. Then the phone is held against the car's NFC reader (a PN532). The phone now behaves exactly like a contactless smart card, a trick called Host Card Emulation (HCE), and the car talks to it with the same short commands a card reader uses (APDUs).

HelloThe car asks "is the Smart-Car key app here?" by selecting its application ID.
Prove the secretA SPAKE2+ exchange: the phone answers a fresh 24-byte challenge with an HMAC that only the right secret can produce.
Swap identitiesThe phone hands over its public key and certificate. The car replies with its own ID and public key.
Prove the keyThe phone signs a challenge with its private key, which never leaves Android's hardware keystore.
CommitThe car saves the phone in owner slot 0, creates an immobilizer token and reports success.

Watch the conversation press Play or click any row

What the car remembers

Everything is stored in the ESP32's flash memory under the name ccc_dk: the car's own ID and key pair, plus eight key slots. Each slot holds a phone's 65-byte public key and a 32-byte immobilizer token.

If an owner is already paired, a new pairing is refused unless the car is put into a special "force" mode.

What the phone keeps

The phone's identity is an elliptic-curve key pair (secp256r1, also called P-256) created inside the Android Keystore under the alias smart_car_phone_identity_p256. The private half can sign but can never be exported.

It is created without a fingerprint or PIN requirement, so the card-emulation service can sign even when the screen is off or the app is in the background.

The phone also remembers which car it was bound to and an optional fast artifact, a 32-byte shared secret that makes later handshakes quicker (see Step 2).

Step 2 · Bluetooth LE · every visit

The handshake: proving who's who

Once paired, the phone never needs to be tapped again. When you come close, it connects over Bluetooth Low Energy. Instead of inventing a new protocol, the car opens a small "tunnel": the phone writes card-style commands into one channel and the car answers on another. Through that tunnel both sides prove their identity and agree on two brand-new session keys that are valid for this visit only.

Standard path

Full key exchange (AUTH0 with P1 = 0x11)

Both sides create a throw-away key pair, sign it with their long-term identity, and swap. Each side checks the other's signature, then combines the throw-away keys (ECDH) into a shared secret that nobody listening can compute.

That secret is stretched with HKDF-SHA256 into a 32-byte ENC key and a 32-byte MAC key.

Fast path

Pre-shared shortcut (AUTH0 with P1 = 0x01)

If the phone handed over a fast artifact during pairing and the rollout settings allow it, the phone just names the artifact version. The car derives the same two session keys straight from that 32-byte secret.

The whole signature-and-ECDH round (AUTH1) is skipped, which saves time at the door.

Both paths end the same way. The car sends a fresh 24-byte challenge (its ID plus 16 random bytes). The phone signs it, and the car checks the signature against the public key saved at pairing. Only then is the session "ready". After that, the phone can request an unlock (CONTROL_FLOW) and start or stop UWB ranging.

Watch the handshake

Under the hood: payloads and key derivation
  • GATT service 0000aaaa-1234-5678-9abc-def012345678; RX (phone writes) 0000aac1-…; TX (car reads/notifies) 0000aac2-….
  • Request frame: [CLA, INS, P1, P2, Lc, data…]. Each response comes back on TX tagged with the original instruction and a status.
  • Car AUTH1: [ecu_ephemeral_pub_65][vehicle_sig_len_le16][vehicle_ECDSA_DER]. Phone AUTH1: [phone_ephemeral_pub_65][phone_sig_len_le16][phone_ECDSA_DER].
  • Standard HKDF: 32-byte ECDH secret, empty salt, info = 16-byte("SmartCarv1|ENC", zero-padded) || ecu_ephemeral_pub_65 || phone_ephemeral_pub_65; MAC uses SmartCarv1|MAC.
  • Fast HKDF: 32-byte artifact, empty salt, info = 16-byte("SmartCarFast|ENC" or "SmartCarFast|MAC") || vehicle_id[8] || artifact_version[1].
  • CONTROL_FLOW, RANGING_START and RANGING_STOP are rejected unless the session keys are ready.

Step 3 · Ultra-Wideband · 10 times a second

Locating the phone

Bluetooth can tell that the phone is somewhere near, but not whether it's at the driver's door or on the other side of the car. Ultra-Wideband (UWB) times how long very short radio pulses take to travel back and forth, which gives a real distance in metres. Three anchors each measure their own distance, and the car turns the three distances into a position.

⚠️ Research transport: The PC bridge (run_fira_bridge.py) is a development-grade serial relay, not a production topology. In a production system this would be replaced by direct SPI/UART between the UWB module and ESP32.

How three circles give one point

Each distance means "the phone is somewhere on this circle". Two circles cross at two points, and the third circle picks the right one. Real measurements are a little noisy, so the car starts from a guess and refines it up to 10 times (Gauss-Newton least squares) until the circles agree as well as possible.

If one anchor drops out, the car still solves with the two remaining circles using a direct formula. Each distance is checked on its own, so one bad anchor doesn't throw away the whole frame.

The Kalman filter, in plain words

Each new fix jumps around a bit. The filter keeps a belief of where you are and how fast you're moving (x, y, vx, vy). When a new fix arrives, it first predicts where you should be now, then blends in the measurement. A good fit (low error) gets more trust and a poor fit gets less.

It never invents positions between measurements. If fixes stop for more than 2 seconds, or one lands more than 2.5 m from the prediction, it simply starts over.

Timing

The anchors range at 10 Hz. Every 100 ms the latest smoothed position is handed to the AI and the access controller. If the newest fix is older than 500 ms, access decisions pause instead of acting on stale data.

Anchor positions (metres, car centre = 0,0, front = +y): A0 (0, −2) rear-centre, A1 (0.95, 0) right B-pillar, A2 (−0.95, 0) left B-pillar.

Under the hood: serial format and filter settings
  • Bridge to car: RANGE:d0=<m>,d1=<m>,d2=<m>,valid=<0|1> and ACK:…. Car to bridge: CMD:START_RANGING, CMD:STOP_RANGING.
  • The car ignores the overall valid flag. Every positive distance sets its own bit in valid_mask.
  • Bridge session: controller 0x06C1, session 42, channel 9, preamble 9; accepted distances 0.1–30.0 m, freshness 500 ms. Example ports COM11 / COM19 / COM12 map to d0 / d1 / d2.
  • EKF: constant-velocity model; measurement noise = fit RMS clamped to 0.05–1.0 m (default 0.15 m); acceleration noise 1.2 m/s²; initial velocity variance 4.0 (m/s)²; speed clamp 2.0 m/s.
  • Incoming frames wait in a queue of depth 8.

Step 4a · TinyML · on the microcontroller

Reading intent from the way you walk

Standing next to the door isn't proof that you want to get in. You might just be walking past. So the car looks at your last 25 movement samples (about 2.5 seconds) and a small neural network, running directly on the ESP32 with TensorFlow Lite Micro, guesses what you're doing.

The model's input: a sliding window illustrative live data

4 rows (x, y, vx, vy) × 25 columns (the newest sample is on the right). Each value is normalised before it enters the model.

Four possible answers

0approach

Walking towards the car. Must reach ≥ 0.80 before an unlock.

1seated

Settled in the driver's seat. Must reach ≥ 0.80 before ignition is allowed.

2leave

Walking away. Must reach ≥ 0.80 before locking an occupied car.

3passing

Just walking by. At ≥ 0.70 an unlock is hard-blocked.

Try the decision rule illustrative numbers

Warming up

The model only answers once all 25 samples are filled. Until then, and whenever the model isn't available, the AI gates are simply skipped: approach, seated and leave count as 1.0 and passing counts as 0.0. The geometry and speed rules still apply.

Small enough for a microcontroller

The whole model runs inside a 44 KiB working memory area, with 32-bit float input and output.

Step 4b · Geofencing + state machine

Deciding when to unlock, start and lock

The car draws invisible zones around itself. A zone only counts once the position has been in it for three samples in a row, which stops a single noisy fix from flipping the zone. Zones are checked from the smallest to the largest: seat first, then door, then welcome, otherwise outside.

Outside

Beyond every other zone.

Welcome

Within 2.5 m. You're nearby, but that's not enough to unlock.

Driver door

Within 1.0 m of the left B-pillar (−0.95, 0).

Driver seat

Within 0.45 m of (−0.35, 0), inside the car.

Three states, four ways to move between them

Unlock (LOCKED → DOOR_UNLOCKED)

At the door or seat · speed under 0.4 m/s · not moving away faster than 0.10 m/s · approach ≥ 0.80 · passing < 0.70 · all true for 3 hits. The door relay then fires for 500 ms.

Sit down (DOOR_UNLOCKED → OCCUPIED)

In the driver's seat · speed under 0.4 m/s · seated ≥ 0.80 · held for a continuous 3 seconds. The door locks and ignition is authorised.

Change of mind (DOOR_UNLOCKED → LOCKED)

Back outside and moving away faster than 0.10 m/s. The car locks immediately, with no extra checks.

Get out and leave (OCCUPIED → LOCKED)

Outside · moving away · leave ≥ 0.80 · 3 hits. Ignition is revoked and the car locks.

Hands-free in the background: the two-worker handoff

Android runs the background service in a separate Dart worker (an "isolate"), while the native UWB API is only reachable from the main app worker. So the job is split: the background worker finds the car and authenticates, then hands the UWB part to the foreground worker.

Firebase · Gemini

The cloud, and catching unusual use

Firebase Authentication identifies the user, and Firestore stores the cars, keys and history. The phone also watches that history for patterns that look wrong, such as an access at 3 a.m., a burst of openings, or a location far from anywhere the car usually goes.

What lives in Firestore

cars/{carId}

Car state plus an owner pairing record: vehicle ID, owner, phone and vehicle public keys, status, slot 0 info.

Vehicles/{vehicleIdHex}

A best-effort mirror of the owner record, keyed by vehicle ID.

digital_keys/{keyId}

Owner, car, name, type, status, permissions, validity window and timestamps.

User/{userId}

The user's profile information.

access_logs/{id}

Who opened which car, when and where. This feeds the anomaly checks.

anomaly_analysis/{id}

Results of the rule-based checks.

anomaly_decisions/{id}

AI-enriched decisions.

never stored

Private keys and immobilizer tokens stay on the phone and in the car.

Sending GPS securely over Bluetooth

The phone shares its location with the car through the authenticated session. The 32 bytes of location data are scrambled with the session ENC key, then a 32-byte fingerprint made with the MAC key is attached, so the car can tell if anything was changed on the way.

ESP32-S3 · FreeRTOS

Inside the car computer

The car computer runs several jobs at the same time using FreeRTOS, a small real-time operating system. Each job is a task with its own priority. When two tasks want the processor, the one with the higher priority wins.

FSM
priority 6
  • core 1 · 8 KB stack
  • runs every 1 ms
  • handles events (queue of 16)
UWB
priority 5
  • core 1 · 20 KB stack
  • reads RANGE / ACK lines
  • tracking + access control, every 5 ms
NFC
priority 4
  • core 1 · 8 KB stack
  • runs every 2 ms
  • drives the pairing session
Main loop
Arduino loop
  • Bluetooth upkeep
  • every 50 ms

The data that moves between parts

One ranging frame

t_ms (uint32), d[3] (double), valid_mask (uint8). Bit i set means distance i is fresh and within bounds.

Tracker state

Position and velocity [px, py, vx, vy], a 4×4 uncertainty matrix, and the times of the last update and the last measurement.

Zones as numbers

OUTSIDE=0, WELCOME=1, DRIVER_DOOR=2, DRIVER_SEAT=3.
